How the lock-in works

For s(t) = A cos(2πft), multiplication by a reference at the same frequency produces a DC term and a component at 2f. The low-pass filter keeps the slowly varying term and suppresses the fast component and much of the broadband noise.

X = LPF[2v(t)cos(2πfᵣt + φ)]
Y = LPF[−2v(t)sin(2πfᵣt + φ)]
R = √(X² + Y²), phase = atan2(Y, X)

With matched frequencies, X ≈ A cos(φ), Y ≈ −A sin(φ), and R ≈ A after settling. Here amplitudes are peak volts; a sine-wave RMS amplitude is R/√2. Hardware instruments may use a different scaling convention.

The one-pole filter uses z[n] = z[n−1] + (1−exp(−Δt/τ))(u[n]−z[n−1]). Its cutoff is 1/(2πτ), its continuous-time equivalent noise bandwidth is 1/(4τ), and a step settles to about 99% in 4.6τ.

A lock-in does not remove all noise. Noise or interference at the reference frequency passes through. A frequency mismatch produces a beat and attenuation. The positive magnitude R has a noise bias, especially when the signal is weak; inspect X and Y as well.

This demo samples at 2000 Hz, uses seeded Gaussian white noise specified as RMS per sample, and starts the filters at zero. The drift is a 0.25 Hz sinusoid. Playback speed changes only the animation, not the calculation. Frequencies are reduced for visualization; in an NV experiment the reference can be the microwave FM frequency and the detected component follows the local ODMR slope.
